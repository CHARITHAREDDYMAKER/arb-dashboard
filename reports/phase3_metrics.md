# Phase 3: metrics

Generated by `python -m src.build_metrics` on 2026-10-10. Money amounts are Rs per quintal unless stated; returns are annualised on a 365-day year.

## What the numbers rest on

- **Spot is validated.** The bhavcopy `UndrlygPric` matched the NCDEX spot price page exactly on 9 Oct 2026 (guar seed Jodhpur 7,706.70 at 16:50; jeera Unjha 24,626.00 at 15:19), a manual check. It is the last polled spot of the day.
- **Storage and assaying are official.** NCCL sheet dated 8 Oct 2026 (`data/reference/`): jeera Rs 0.70 per quintal per day and Rs 50 per quintal assaying; guar seed Rs 0.45 and Rs 15. Storage includes insurance and fumigation.
- **Exchange fee, SEBI fee, stamp duty, CTT exemption and GST on brokerage are sourced** (see `config/carry_costs.toml`).
- **Two assumptions remain**, and every number that depends on them is marked †:
  1. Handling (loading and unloading): Rs 10 per quintal, a placeholder, not sourced.
  2. GST treatment of warehouse charges: 18% applied to storage and assaying as the conservative case; not verified.
- Brokerage (Rs 10 per lakh) and the funding spread (1% over T-bill) are trader inputs, adjustable in the dashboard.
- The last 5 trading days use the RBI auction yield because FBIL had not yet published; they are flagged `tbill_is_rbi_fill`.
- Not modelled: repository and repository-participant charges, minimum holding periods for storage, margin funding on the futures leg, mandi fees and transport on the spot purchase, and quality premiums or discounts at delivery.

## How to read the two return measures

- **Net profit** deducts every cost including funding at T-bill plus the spread. Positive means the trade beats borrowing.
- **Cash-and-carry return** is the annualised return on the cash invested after physical and transaction costs but before funding. It is the figure to compare with the T-bill yield; funding is left out so it is not counted twice.
- **Raw signals** compare futures with fair value (spot plus funding plus storage) and ignore the one-off assaying and handling costs. A signal is **tradeable** only if the trade also makes money after all costs. Headlines and the opportunity log use the tradeable signal.

## Three findings for the interview

### 1. The carry is in the calendar spread, not in spot versus the near month

The futures curve was in contango on 91.9% of days for jeera and 99.6% for guar seed, sloping up about 1.4% and 1.5% a month (roughly 17% and 18% a year), well above the cost of money plus warehouse rent. Yet the contract nearest to expiry trades at or below spot: average basis 6 to 10 days before expiry is -0.53% for jeera and -0.63% for guar seed, against +2.03% and +1.43% at 46 to 60 days. A contract loses its premium as it approaches delivery. The practical reading: enter cash-and-carry early, 30 to 60 days out, or hold stock and sell the next month, and avoid the expiring contract.

### 2. Guar seed is a reverse-arb market; jeera is two-sided

Guar seed futures sat below fair value on 64% of days and cash-and-carry made money after all costs on only 14% †. Jeera split almost evenly: a tradeable cash-and-carry on 43% of days and a tradeable reverse arb on 44% †. The reverse side persists because it cannot be arbitraged from scratch: physical goods cannot be borrowed and sold short, so only someone already holding stock can sell spot and buy futures. A discount in futures is a payment to stockists, not free money.

### 3. The model is tight where it can be checked, and size is the real limit

Final settlement prices match the 3-day average of the spot series to within 0.07% on average across 23 expired contracts, and the spot series matched the exchange's spot page exactly on the date checked by hand. So a position held to delivery converges as modelled; the residual risk is quality, delivery logistics and the 3-day averaging window, not price. What limits the trade is liquidity: at 10% of average volume the active contract absorbs about Rs 2.9 crore a day in jeera and Rs 10.7 crore in guar seed. Fixed costs matter too: assaying and handling are paid once, so they weigh heavily on a 10-day trade and little on a 60-day one.
